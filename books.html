When the Porch Light Stays On

By Amara St. James

Three generations gather in a Brooklyn house where every room keeps a different version of the family story.

Read the opening

The porch light had been on for three days before anybody admitted they were waiting. Amara’s mother called it a habit. Her grandmother called it good manners. The youngest child in the house, who had no reason to protect anyone’s version of the truth, called it hope.

Saltwater Inheritance

By Malik Baptiste

River, language, and lineage meet in poems about what a family carries and what it chooses to release.

Read the opening

The river did not ask what we had named ourselves. It moved past the house carrying weather, branches, and the small shining things we were too slow to keep. My father said inheritance was what remained. My mother said it was what we learned to hear.

The City Keeps a Chorus

By Nia Caldwell

A Washington neighborhood becomes an unlikely chorus when its residents begin recording the stories of a changing block.

Read the opening

At six every morning, Mrs. Avery opened her window to the same block and a different conversation. The city on television had never looked like the one below her sill. She began, one Tuesday, to write down the difference.

Cypress & Memory

By Celeste Landry

Family letters and remembered conversations open a layered portrait of Louisiana Creole life.

Read the opening

The first letter arrived folded inside a photograph. No one could remember who had placed it there. The handwriting was familiar to everyone and claimed by no one, as if the family had invented a shared hand for the things it could not say aloud.

Winter Has a Second Name

By Imani Okafor

A collection of northern light, Black belonging, and the warmth found in unexpected company.

Read the opening

In winter, the street kept its own quiet. I learned to distinguish it from loneliness. Quiet had footsteps in it. Quiet had the scrape of a neighbor’s shovel and the porch light that appeared before I turned the corner.

Mango Season, Again

By Simone Laurent

A Miami family reunion unsettles the stories everyone thought they already knew.

Read the opening

By the time the mangoes ripened, everyone had decided the reunion would be simple. The table would fit. The stories would behave. Nobody would ask about the years missing from the photographs. Simone knew better, but she set out the plates anyway.
